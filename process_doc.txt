Discussion Questions
Based on the results of your ‘think aloud’ study, what would you improve in your data
story?
​
Based on the results of the think-aloud study, the data story needs clearer narrative
communication, more consistent design decisions, and simpler, more interpretable visuals.
Although both testers grasped the general idea that transfers have shifted in the NIL era, they
did not feel the main takeaway was stated clearly enough. They indicated the need to introduce
NIL context earlier and explicitly explain key claims. Visual clarity can be improved by adjusting
crowded plots, unclear highlighting, and ambiguous interactions. Consistency can be improved
by ensuring that class year is ordered correctly and color schemes are cohesive. Testers also
emphasized the importance of clearer interface elements, like labeling the dropdown and
making visual changes in the final graphic more stable and clearly tied to the data. Together
these refinements would strengthen comprehension and ensure the central message is
immediately visible.
Are there any additional insights and visualizations you would use? Would you amplify
or change your message? Did your narrative work? Did the tester get your takeaways?
​
The think-aloud results suggest adding a few targeted insights and potentially
introducing one new visualization to strengthen clarity rather than expanding the dashboard.
One opportunity is to emphasize the pattern both testers noticed, that juniors transfer more
often, by surfacing it more directly through annotations. A simplified categorical breakdown,
such as the suggested pie chart, could replace the bar chart if testing shows it reduces cognitive
load. The focus should remain on clarifying existing visuals rather than adding more. The core
message should stay the same, but it needs to be amplified. The narrative should state upfront
how transfer behavior changed before and after NIL, explain what 21% faster is comparing, and
intentionally highlight only a few meaningful examples so viewers understand why they matter.
The narrative partially worked. Both testers understood that the story was about transfer
acceleration in the NIL era, but they did not fully grasp the intended takeaways, indicating that
the message was implied rather than clearly delivered. Strengthening explanatory text,
standardizing class-year ordering and color schemes, reducing visual crowding, and adding
basic interactions like hover and line highlighting would make the central insights more explicit
and ensure that users not only notice the patterns but actually understand them.

Innovative view design
Our VarsityViz project goes beyond generic visualizations with designs selected to appeal to our
target audience. Specifically, we plan to implement a newspaper-style layout for our hook
visualization. This layout appeals to athletic department directors who may be used to reading
classic sports newspapers, while also giving the feel that the audience is reading ‘breaking NIL
news.’ We plan to continue to use innovative designs throughout the website, with
stoplight-inspired chart designs and solutions laid out in the style of a coach’s whiteboard, sure
to tap into the emotions and focus coaches and teammates have viewing similar whiteboards
before games.
Additionally, we go beyond static visualizations with plans for filtering and layering callout boxes
to keep the audience engaged and improve understanding. For instance, we intend to use filters
to allow users to view NCAA-level trends or drill down to the individual sport level, perhaps to
sports that most appeal to them. We are also considering supporting conference-level views.
Lastly, we plan to use callout boxes that appear when hovering over critical areas, such as
datapoints, reducing the need for confusing legends and offering hidden insights into the data.

Combined with our brief, snappy story telling lines interspersed through our charts, our website
offers busy athletic department staff a relatable and interactive way to learn about data-driven
insights impacting their school.

Summary of Rationale
We selected sketches (i.e., figures) 4, 9, 10, 15, and 16 because they give athletic departments a holistic
view of student athlete transfer behavior both from NCAA-wide perspective and from the perspective of
one of the highest revenue-generating sports: football. We all voted for Figure 10, as it clearly shows what
schools student athletes are transferring to most, providing athletic departments with a simple starting
point for selecting schools to further investigate. Figure 9 provides a similar answer, but with an artistic,
memorable flair and a focus on football player transfers. Figures 4, 15 and 16 were selected given their
clever visualization of the rich CFB dataset, allowing athletic departments to answer multiple questiuons
at once and dig deeper into trends behind football player transfers. Specifically, these three sketches help
identify schools that have a higher influx of highly-rated players, highlight schools that have unusually
large or small transfer rates, and explain how school transfer behavior changes over time. Overall, these
sketches focus on the transfer-related questions we imagine to be top priority for athletic directors:
questions related to the impact of NIL over time and the quality and quantity of transfers in and out of
schools.

CFP Dataset:
The College Football transfers dataset contains 6,547 rows, with 69.3% having at least one
missing value. The most significant missingness occurs in rating (65.5%) and destination
(38.4%) columns, while stars has only 8.1% missing. The rest of the data appears to be without
missing values. While there are no exact duplicate rows, analysis of the composite key
(first_name, last_name, transfer_date) revealed 4 pairs of potential duplicates. Text quality
issues include non-ASCII characters in name fields and case variations, particularly in
first_name (14 variations). Key transformations needed: parse transfer_date strings into proper
datetime format, standardize text fields by handling non-ASCII characters and case
normalization, and create a normalized composite key for deduplication.

NCAA Dataset:
After confirming the NCAA data was already clean with zero missing values and no duplicates,
we identified a structural inefficiency: the wide format with year-stamped columns created
barriers for temporal analysis and cross-year comparisons. Each year-measure combination
occupied its own column (e.g., "2022 Graduate", "2023 Undergraduate"), forcing analysts to
manually specify column names for filtering or aggregation. We transformed all four datasets
from wide to long format, where year, measure type, and value became explicit variables rather
than being embedded in column headers. This restructuring was very important for Q4, where
we decomposed complex column names into three separate dimensions (entrant_type,
outcome, and value_type), enabling independent filtering on aid status and transfer outcomes.

CFP Dataset:

The CFP transfer dataset required five cleaning steps to prepare it for analysis. We handled
missing values by adding a has_rating flag for missing ratings, replacing missing destinations
with "Uncommitted", and preserving missing star ratings. We identified and removed 4 duplicate
player entries, prioritizing records with ratings when available. Text standardization cleaned
names by removing non-ASCII characters, converted positions to uppercase, and formatted
school names consistently. Date parsing transformed transfer_date into proper datetime format
and generated temporal features (transfer_year, transfer_month, transfer_quarter) plus a
post_nil flag marking transfers after July 2021. Finally, we standardized eligibility as categorical
text and added an eligible_immediately flag for quick filtering. The cleaned dataset contains
6,543 rows with 7 newly engineered features.

​
Our original vision was to explore how the NCAA’s NIL policy may have reshaped trends
in student-athlete transfers. Our goal was to visualize these shifts across sports, schools, and
time. The datasets we settled on, the NCAA aggregated transfer data and a College Football
Transfer dataset found on Kaggle, lined up well with this vision. The NCAA dataset gives us
clean, institution-level counts across sports. This is ideal for broad policy and trend questions.
The Kaggle dataset provides us with individual-level football transfer entries. It includes
attributes like ratings, positions, and specific schools. Together, these sources allow us to
explore both macro- and micro-level trends about transfers before and after the NIL rules were
put in place. Some of our original questions regarding comparing NIL effects across all sports or
linking policy changes to individual player decisions are more difficult to answer as the NCAA is
aggregated and the football-specific data does include direct NIL indicators.
​
The biggest data quality challenge we faced was cleaning and reshaping the Kaggle
dataset. Nearly 70% of the rows had at least one missing value, specifically in ratings and
destinations. This required us to flag or reformat variables for consistent analysis. We also had
to standardize text fields, remove duplicates, and parse dates into formats that are more usable.
Conversely, the NCAA data was already clean but was in a wide format that made analysis
more difficult. We transformed it into a tidier long format to make our work simpler in the
long-run. These steps highlighted how much effort is required to prepare real-world data before
it can be visualized. All in all, our datasets are strong foundations for analyzing conference-level
trends, position-based movement in football, and broad shifts caused by changes in NIL policy.
That said, certain questions regarding athlete motivations and NIL effects may be out of reach
without more detailed or individual-level data.

Reflection on Visualizations - Kate Gilliam
The visualizations above address questions related to how transfer activity varies
across conferences, schools, and athlete experience levels. In Figure 5, we use a bar
graph to show transfer activity by conference. This provides a starting point for
comparing policies and competitive environments across conferences. In Figure 6, we
present a map visualization of transfers from school to school. This makes geographic
patterns in athlete movement more visible and shows trends in how location or regional
rivalries might influence transfer decisions. In Figure 7, we show transfers by grade
level. This displays when athletes are most likely to leave their current program. This

shows trends in pressure points in recruitment and retention. At this stage, it is difficult
to tell which of these perspectives is the most informative, as these visualizations
represent only a preliminary analysis. That said, these visualizations do provide athletic
directors with information that may spark further investigation. For example, they might
focus on conferences with unusually high transfer activity, or examine grade-level
support systems for athletes who are most likely to transfer. Ultimately, these
visualizations are a good foundation for further exploration. They offer athletic
departments a clearer picture of transfer dynamics while also pointing to more questions
that will guide further analysis.

Abstract
Student-athlete transfers are an important factor in collegiate sports, having major
impacts on recruiting strategy, team performance, and athletes’ performance. In July
2021, NCAA adopted the name/image/likeness (NIL) policy, which allows
student-athletes to receive financial compensation and representation. Our team
postulates this policy may have shifted the transfer landscape dramatically. In this
project, we aim to explore the landscape of student-athlete transfers in America through
three main goals:
1) investigate whether the NIL policy correlates with changes in transfer behavior,
2) explore whether certain features are predictive of transfers, and
3) visualize the broader transfer landscape across geography and time.
We plan to use either the NCAA’s official transfer data or a rich Kaggle dataset focusing
on collegiate football transfers. By using maps to show where athletes are transferring
to and from, our visualizations will allow for easy processing of rich geographical data.
We also plan to use time series and multivariate views to show trends and patterns
across geographical space and time. We hope these visualizations will not only tell the
broader story of collegiate transfers, but also help inform coaches, recruiters, athletes,
and sports enthusiasts.
Background and Motivation
Our team of current and former student athletes selected the general topic of athletics
as a way to bring our passion for sports into the classroom. In July 2021, the NCAA
adopted a policy allowing student athletes to receive compensation for their
name/image/likeness (NIL) (NCAA, 2025). This watershed moment/landmark legislation
sparked our interest in the specific topic of athlete transfers. This legislation raises
questions such as: How did the landscape of student-athlete transfers shift after it
became legal for schools to offer financial incentives to student-athletes? Our original
idea was to focus on Harvard student athletes; however, we found institutional policy
restricted our access to the necesary data. We are currently considering
publicly-available data sources regarding the topic.
Data
Our team is currently considering online, publicly-available student athlete transfer
datasets. We are considering using the NCAA’s dataset, since we expect
student-athletes within the NCAA to be the most likely to be affected by the NIL policy
shift, and since we are interested in observing shifts in transfer behavior across sports
(Transfer Portal Data: Division I Student-Athlete Transfer Trends, 2025). This dataset
would most likely need to be manually entered into a spreadsheet; no preprocessing
would be needed given how the data is already cleaned and in aggregate. We are also
considering a college football dataset available on kaggle given the dataset’s richness

and opportunity to explore a larger variety of data potentially related to collegiate
transfers (College Football Portal & Recruiting Statistics, 2023). This dataset would be
downloaded as a .csv file. Preprocessing, such as consolidation of schools based on
geographic region, may be conducted as a way to better elucidate trends.
